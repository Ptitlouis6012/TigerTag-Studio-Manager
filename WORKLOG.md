# Worklog — v2.23.2 (in progress)

## Added
- **A machine's camera, on its own card** — a play key over the printer's photo, and pressing it swaps the photo for the live feed in the same frame. The frame is the SAME whether a feed is running or not: the photo box used to be sized by the image inside it, so a card grew the moment you pressed play and every card below it shifted. It now states the height a feed needs, always, and the photo is simply centred in it — pressing play swaps the contents and moves nothing. Measured on a real board: 155 px per card before and after starting a stream. The feed goes straight into the photo box and is sized exactly as the camera wall sizes its own — same containers, same per-brand elements, same 16:9 — rather than through rules invented for the board: that view already answers "a feed in a box that is not the side panel", including the 200px floor the shared container carries for the panel and the letterboxing each brand chooses on purpose. One rule the wall never needed had to be added: the photo box sizes any image to its intrinsic size, which is right for a product shot and wrong for a feed — the picture sat small and centred inside a correctly-shaped frame, and only a measurement told the two apart. The key only appears on a machine that can actually be watched: building the feed is also the availability test, so a brand or model without a camera never offers it. FlashForge gets the camera wall's banner rather than the side panel's: it holds ONE stream at a time, and the panel's banner opens that stream itself — several cards, or a card and an open panel, would fight over it. The wall's is an empty image fed by the shared multiplexer, so every consumer is served by one upstream connection; the board is a wall too. (The panel's also carries a fixed element id, which cannot be right on a view that draws one per machine.) Pressing play WAKES the machine's link first, one idempotent kick per brand, exactly as the camera wall does before it draws — a feed cannot be shown that nothing has asked for, and Bambu in particular streams only on request, which is why its picture used to appear solely after opening the side panel. The link is rarely up by the time play is released, so play asks and the picture is put in place as soon as there is one: a catch-up runs on the same tick that patches the rest of the board and does nothing once the feed is in the DOM. That is what lets a card fill itself instead of waiting for a full redraw. Pressing play anywhere lights BOTH the card and the widget for that machine — it is one intent, and the shared multiplexer keeps the two views to a single upstream connection — `renderer/inventory.js`, `renderer/css/40-printers.css`
- **A camera widget on the board** — the machine's live feed, placeable like anything else, in a 16:9 frame that keeps its size whether the machine is there or not. It is the one widget you ASK for: switched off by default, and even once shown it stays quiet until you press play. Every other widget is free to draw, but a camera opens a connection per machine — drawn on sight, a board of ten printers opened ten streams the moment it appeared, and the unreachable ones spent the whole time retrying. So it can be placed, sized and arranged for nothing, and shows its feed when asked, one machine at a time. Pressing play is not stored: wanting to look now is not a setting, and a board reopened tomorrow is quiet again. It reuses the dispatcher the side panel and the camera wall already use, so no transport is reimplemented: MJPEG, WebRTC and iframe arrive solved, and the shared multiplexer means a feed shown in two places still opens ONE upstream connection. Its frame is stated outright from the board's card width rather than as a percentage: the card sizes itself to its content, so a percentage inside it is circular — resolved as `auto`, contributing nothing, and an aspect-ratio computed from a zero width gives a zero height. It is the strictest case of the live-media rule: its body holds a stream, so it is rebuilt ONLY when the machine comes or goes — a feed replaced on every tick would stutter for ever — and it re-attaches itself afterwards — `renderer/inventory.js`, `renderer/css/40-printers.css`
- **A print widget on the board** — the panel's job block, placeable like anything else: preview, file name, percentage, progress bar, state, remaining time, finish clock and the layer count. It reads the same normalised job the table and the card already use, so no brand's wording reaches it; the six firmwares' six names for the layer pair (`layerNum`, `currLayer`, `printLayerCur`…) are folded into one there rather than at each call site. With no preview to show — an idle machine, or a firmware that offers none — it falls back to the printer's own photo through the same chain the panel's hero uses, drawn whole rather than cropped and set back a little: an empty grey square said nothing, while the machine at least says whose widget this is. It stays when the machine is silent, dimmed with dashes, like the temperatures — a dashboard the user arranged must not rearrange itself because a printer went quiet. It holds an image, so it supplies its own refresh: each field is written on its own and the preview only when its URL genuinely changes, since replacing the whole body on every percent would re-set the background image — the table had already learned that. Adding it was one entry in the widget table plus its CSS, which is what that table was rebuilt for — `renderer/inventory.js`, `renderer/css/40-printers.css`
- **Pause and stop on a machine's board card**, while a print is running — the two controls its own panel offers, brought to where you look when you watch a fleet. They stack down the RIGHT edge of the photo, mirroring the brand badge on the left, rather than joining the head's row: that row is what the app can do to the card — move it, open its menu — and mixing it with what the machine can be told to do made neither read. They name their printer through the card itself rather than relying on which panel happens to be open: the panel has one machine, the board has all of them, and the existing handlers were all bound to the open one. Every brand now answers the same `controlJob(printer, action)`, so nothing on the board knows a protocol — six drivers, six three-line adapters over the transports they already had. They wear the panel's own colours — blue for pause and resume, red for stop — so the same two controls look the same wherever you meet them; the tint is composited over an opaque plate rather than over transparency, because on the board they sit on a photo. Stop is **hold-to-confirm**: it ends a print that may be hours in, and a board shows many machines at once, so a stray click on the wrong card must not be enough. The key turns over in place when the machine pauses — icon, action and label — while a print starting or ending goes through the board's own rebuild, since that changes the card's shape. No new i18n: the panel's own Pause / Resume / Cancel wording is reused — `renderer/inventory.js`, `renderer/printers/*/index.js`, `renderer/css/40-printers.css`

## Changed
- The widget switches in a machine's ⋮ are ordered Print, Temperature, Filament storage, Camera — what you reach for most, first, with the one that costs a connection last. The list simply follows the widget table now, and a note says so: nothing reads that table by position, so its order is free to mean something — `renderer/inventory.js`
- **A group's outline is shown only while something is being moved.** At rest the rings describe a state you already know, and on a board with several bound machines they cover it in orange, competing with the cards they exist to serve. During a move the ring is the one thing you need — it says which objects are coming with you — and every ring appears, not just the one you took, so you can also see what you are about to land among. They fade rather than blink, and the layout keeps patching them in place — `renderer/css/40-printers.css`
- The **filament widget is drawn even when the machine has reported nothing** — a placeholder card with one unknown bay, dimmed like any quiet widget. It used to draw nothing at all, so a user who switched it on had it switched on and nowhere to be seen: it could not be placed, and the arrangement had to be redone once the printer finally connected. Three separate gates asked the same wrong question — the render, the live patch, and the list of a machine's board objects — and the last one is why it also stayed out of its machine's group. Being switched ON is what makes it one of the machine's objects, not whether the machine has said anything yet — `renderer/inventory.js`

## Fixed
- **A feed opened correctly, then turned into a broken-image icon a second later.** Replacing a stream tears the old one down, and a teardown blanks its consumers and revokes the frame they are showing — right when a stream really ends, wrong when it is merely being replaced: the very elements just carried over to the new stream were emptied and their picture revoked underneath them. The last frame is now handed over with them, so a restart is invisible — the previous picture simply stays until the new one arrives — `renderer/printers/cam_manager.js`
- **A camera went black for seconds on every change of view**, even though the app was holding the feed a moment earlier. A stream survived only two seconds without a watcher — less than a view change takes — so it died between the two and the arriving view rebuilt it from nothing: a fresh connection, then the wait for a first frame. It now outlives a switch, so the new view subscribes to a LIVE stream and is handed the last frame at once. The camera is still released when you actually stop looking — it is simply no longer released between two views that both want it — `renderer/printers/cam_manager.js`
- **A camera that failed to start was never retried.** A printer accepting ONE client — FlashForge's does — refuses every attempt made in the gap between a view handing the stream back and the printer actually closing it, and switching between two views lands in that gap almost every time. Nothing retried, and a stream is only ever re-asked for by a render, which the side panel does once and never again: the arriving view stayed black for good while the one that got there first kept the picture. A failed stream now retries while a consumer is still on screen, and stops on its own once none is — `renderer/printers/cam_manager.js`
- **A camera feed reached only whichever surface registered for it LAST.** Restarting a stream handed the new one an empty consumer set, and every surface registers once — when it opens — so it never learned the stream underneath had been replaced. A side panel opened while the feed was failing, or a card already on screen when another view started it, went black for good while the newcomer had the picture. The consumers are carried across a restart now, minus any element no longer in the document — and that prune is also what finally lets a stream notice its last consumer has gone, which is the condition that arms its stop and releases a single-client camera — `renderer/printers/cam_manager.js`
- The board never handed its camera consumers back to the shared stream manager — neither on a rebuild nor, worse, on LEAVING the printer view. That manager keeps consumers in a set and never prunes detached ones, so the stream never saw its last consumer go, which is what arms its stop. A FlashForge feed started from the board would have kept that printer's single client slot for ever, so the camera wall could not have it afterwards: one card with a picture, the other black. The board now releases them before every redraw AND before branching away to the table or the wall, as the side panel already does on close — `renderer/inventory.js`
- A board widget needing post-render wiring was wired only when switched on, never after a full render — so it worked once and was dead after the next rebuild: a view change, a reload, any brand tick that redraws the board. The hook was documented and half-implemented; it now runs after a full render too. Invisible until a widget actually needed it, which is what a camera does — `renderer/inventory.js`
- A widget on the printer board changed width depending on where it stood: a Snapmaker's five temperature pills sat on one row in the middle of the board and folded onto two near the right edge, the card resizing under the pointer as it was dragged. It was sized `fit-content`, which on an absolutely positioned box is capped by the space left to the container's right edge — so the same content asked for a different width in a different place. `max-content` asks only what the content needs, and the board grows to hold it, which is what a plan you pan around is for — `renderer/css/40-printers.css`
- A widget switched on for a printer that has never connected stayed loose instead of binding to its machine, so moving the machine left it behind — while a connected machine's widgets travelled with it as they should. Both the position and the binding are written by the same adoption step, and that step waited for the machine's `units` map to arrive before trusting an absent position. Right for a single unit, whose position lives INSIDE that map; wrong for a widget, whose position is a plain field on the machine's own document and arrives with the machine. A never-connected printer has no units, so its widgets could never be adopted at all. The test now matches where each object's position actually lives — `renderer/inventory.js`
- The widgets of a printer that has never connected could not be swept into a selection, while every card around them selected normally. The rubber band hit-tests against each object's RECORDED position, and a widget only records one once its machine has reported — a guard that exists so an incomplete read cannot overwrite a real arrangement, but which keys on data a never-connected machine has none of. Its widgets were therefore laid out, visible and un-sweepable. The band now falls back to where an object is DRAWN when the record has none: that is a position too, and it is the one the user is pointing at. Both boards — `renderer/inventory.js`
- The colour finder — the rainbow and the grey ramp — was drawn over the camera wall, offering to select a spool colour on a screen that holds no spools. It was hidden on the printer views by the same switch that hides the printer FILTERS, and the wall drops those deliberately (there is nothing to filter on a wall of live feeds), so dropping one brought the other back. The wall now says so itself and the two concerns stop sharing a switch — `renderer/inventory.js`, `renderer/css/30-racks.css`
